## How to Choose Dog Food on Chewy

Before comparing brands, identify the feeding problem you are trying to solve. A recipe designed for a growing puppy is not interchangeable with food intended for a senior dog. Likewise, a “sensitive stomach” formula is not the same as a veterinary diet for a diagnosed medical condition.

### 1. Start with life stage

Most products are labeled for one of the following:

- Puppy or growth
- Adult maintenance
- Senior
- All life stages

Puppy food is formulated for growth and usually has different calorie, protein, mineral, and feeding requirements than adult maintenance food. Large-breed puppies may need a formula with controlled calcium and phosphorus levels, so choosing by age alone is not always enough.

Adult dogs generally need a complete and balanced maintenance diet. Senior formulas can be useful for older dogs, but “senior” is not a universal nutritional standard. Read the product details rather than assuming every senior recipe has the same calorie level or joint-support ingredients.

### 2. Match the food to body size

Small dogs often need smaller kibble and more calorie-dense meals because they eat less food by weight. Large dogs may benefit from larger kibble, feeding guidance designed for their size, or formulas that account for joint and mobility concerns.

The bag size can also mislead you. A 15-pound bag may look inexpensive, but the price per pound could be higher than a 30-pound bag of a comparable formula. Chewy typically displays price-per-pound or price-per-ounce information on product listings, which makes comparison easier.

### 3. Look for a complete and balanced diet

The most important information is not always the marketing language on the front of the bag. Look for the nutritional adequacy statement on the product page or packaging.

A complete diet should be appropriate for the dog’s life stage and intended as a main food. Treats, toppers, and supplemental products should not automatically replace a complete meal.

Ingredient lists are useful, but they should be read alongside the guaranteed analysis, calorie information, feeding instructions, and adequacy statement. A recipe with an appealing ingredient list can still be a poor match if it is too calorie-dense for your dog or is not designed for the correct life stage.

### 4. Treat “sensitive stomach” claims carefully

Sensitive-stomach dog food can mean several different things:

- A recipe with moderate fat
- A limited-ingredient formula
- A recipe using a different protein source
- Food with added fiber or probiotics
- A veterinary gastrointestinal diet

Those are not identical. If your dog has repeated vomiting, diarrhea, unexplained weight loss, blood in the stool, or persistent appetite changes, a retail “sensitive stomach” recipe may not address the cause. A veterinarian should help determine whether a therapeutic diet or medical evaluation is needed.

For a mild feeding transition problem, switching gradually over several days is usually more sensible than changing foods suddenly. The exact transition period depends on the dog and the food, but mixing the old and new foods in increasing proportions can reduce digestive disruption.

## Dry, Wet, Fresh, or Raw: Which Format Makes Sense?

### Dry dog food

Dry kibble is usually the most economical format for everyday feeding. It is easy to portion, stores well, and is available in nearly every life-stage and health-oriented category.

Chewy’s current dog food listings show mainstream dry food examples ranging from budget formulas to premium recipes. For example, the current “best dog food” page lists products such as Pedigree Complete Nutrition at $25.97 for a 40-pound bag, Purina ONE Digestive & Gut Health Support at $59.00 for a 40-pound bag, and Hill’s Science Diet Adult Small Bites at $86.99 for a 35-pound bag. These prices are product-specific and can change.

Dry food is a reasonable starting point when:

- Your dog eats kibble willingly.
- You need predictable storage.
- Cost per meal matters.
- You want to use an Autoship schedule.
- Your dog does not require a prescription or specialized feeding format.

### Wet dog food

Wet food can improve palatability and add moisture. It is also useful as a topper for dogs that lose interest in dry kibble.

The tradeoff is cost and storage. Cans, trays, and pouches often cost more per calorie than dry food, and an opened container needs refrigeration. If you use wet food as a topper, account for those calories so your dog does not gain weight unintentionally.

### Fresh and refrigerated food

Fresh food is usually sold frozen or refrigerated and often uses recognizable meat, vegetables, grains, or other whole-food ingredients. Chewy’s fresh-food listings include Get Real, Freshpet, JustFoodForDogs, Instinct, Stella & Chewy’s, Royal Canin Fresh, and other brands.

Current listings show examples such as Freshpet Vital at $41.99 for a 5.5-pound bag, Instinct FreshRaw Bites at $44.99 for a 6-pound bag, and Get Real recipes starting at $27.30, with final pricing depending on the recipe and selected format.

Fresh food may be worth considering when:

- Your dog strongly prefers fresh texture.
- You can manage freezer or refrigerator space.
- You are comfortable with the recurring cost.
- The meal plan fits your dog’s calorie needs.
- You want a portion-based feeding system.

The phrase “human-grade” describes ingredient and production standards; it is not, by itself, proof that a food is nutritionally superior for every dog. Check the complete-and-balanced statement and the intended life stage.

### Raw and freeze-dried food

Raw and freeze-dried products can be more expensive and require closer attention to storage, handling, rehydration, and feeding instructions. Freeze-dried food may be served dry or rehydrated, while frozen raw products may need thawing.

For households with children, immunocompromised family members, or limited food-storage space, handling requirements deserve as much attention as the ingredient list. The best choice is the food that fits both the dog’s nutritional needs and the household’s ability to use it safely and consistently.

## How to Compare the Real Cost

Use this quick method before adding a product to your cart:

1. Check the package weight or total ounces.
2. Divide the price by the weight to estimate cost per pound or ounce.
3. Compare the feeding guide for your dog’s weight.
4. Check the calorie content if it is listed.
5. Add shipping, taxes, and any recurring-order discount.
6. Confirm whether a promotion applies to the exact product and customer account.
7. Review the price of future Autoship deliveries before placing the first order.

A lower cost per pound does not always mean a lower daily feeding cost. A calorie-dense food may require a smaller daily portion, while a less calorie-dense formula may require more food. The most useful figure is the approximate cost per day, not only the shelf price.
